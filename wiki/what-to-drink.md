## The beer styles that make this trip interesting

| Style / experience | What to notice | Where it fits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Northern/session bitter** | A dry, moreish pint, often low in strength; hop bitterness, restrained fruit, toast or biscuit, and an aftertaste that invites another sip rather than a sugar finish. | Compare a Manchester house bitter, a Robinsons traditional beer in Stockport, and a local Merseyside bitter. |
| **Best bitter / ESB** | A step up in malt body, strength and sometimes caramel, balanced by hop bitterness. The letters **ESB** are a style/name clue, not a promise of a specific recipe or character. | A useful contrast if Marble's **Rocket** or **Extra Special Marble** is on. |
| **Mild** | Don't assume "mild" means bland or necessarily dark. Dark mild can be a joyful mix of cocoa, toast, nuts and light roast without the sharp bitterness of a stout. | Ask for a local mild at [City Arms](venues/city-arms.md), [Petersgate Tap](venues/petersgate-tap-stockport.md) or [Fox & Pine](venues/fox-and-pine-oldham.md) if a verified cask is available. |
| **Porter and stout** | Compare roasted grain, chocolate and coffee against sweetness and mouthfeel. Neither "black" nor "strong" alone identifies the style. | Seek a dark **changing cask** at a specialist bar, or Marble Stout if available. |
| **Traditional strong/old ale** | Dried fruit, warming alcohol, complex malt, sometimes a vinous character. A **half** may be the right measure. | **Robinsons Old Tom 8.5%**, particularly pertinent to the Stockport excursion, if cask remains after the **7 Nov** launch. |
| **Modern cask pale / IPA** | Brighter citrus, stone fruit or tropical hops with cask's softer carbonation; compare a good cask version with a keg counterpart rather than assuming they should taste identical. | [Café Beermoth](venues/cafe-beermoth.md), [Port Street Beer House](venues/port-street-beer-house.md), [Gas Lamp](venues/gas-lamp.md), [Fell](venues/fell-northern-quarter.md). |
| **Mixed-culture / barrel beer** | Look for lactic acidity, funk, fruit, oak and restrained oxidation where appropriate; *not* a beer-quality fault automatically. | [Balance Brewing & Blending](venues/balance-brewing-blending.md) is a specialist detour, **not a default cask pub**. Confirm its short opening window. |

Style terms are not regulated tasting guarantees; brewer, batch, storage and your glass determine the experience. CAMRA's [beer-style education resources](https://members.camra.org.uk/learn-discover/the-basics/beer-styles/) and [cask-conditioned guide](https://members.camra.org.uk/learn-discover/the-basics/what-is-cask-conditioned-ale/) are useful background.

## How to judge a cask pint, not just a brewery logo

Cask ale conditions in the **serving vessel**; the cellarman's skill and the speed of sale matter. If you taste a house bitter in several pubs, compare *the same beer* for mouthfeel, carbonation, expression of hops and obvious faults rather than simply adding every unique badge to a drinking app. If a sample is clearly off, politely tell staff.

- **Appearance:** clarity can vary legitimately, especially with intentionally hazy styles; haze alone is not a verdict. A flat, tired appearance alongside a dull flavour may call for a question about condition.
- **Aroma:** fresh hop/malt expression is enjoyable; overt vinegar, overwhelming buttery/butterscotch flavours or musty cardboard where inappropriate deserve attention. A tiny amount of diacetyl can be stylistically tolerated in some British ales, so don't apply an absolute rule by smell alone.
- **Texture and temperature:** a gently lively, well-integrated cask pint served around cellar temperature is different from a warm, lifeless one. Cask is not supposed to taste like cold, aggressively carbonated keg.
- **Turnover:** fewer beautifully conditioned casks can beat a wall of neglected pumps. Ask staff "which cask has been especially good this week?" and whether the mild/porter is selling regularly.
- **Dispense isn't proof:** some **brewery-conditioned "Fresh Ale"** products are drawn through handpumps. CAMRA explicitly flags the issue. Look for the *specific* product and ask whether it is actually **cask-conditioned**, not merely dispensed from a beer engine. [CAMRA Handpump Hijack guidance](https://camra.org.uk/take-action/handpump-hijack).

## Cider and perry: taste the fruit, verify the product

**Perry is made from pears, not apples**; a named traditional perry can be light, floral, delicate or tannic depending on pear varieties and method. A cider made with bittersweet cider apples can be dry-feeling and structured by **tannin**, even if it has some residual sugar. **Acidity** adds freshness and bite, and is a different sensation from tannin's drying grip. Try comparing **a bittersweet, tannic cider**, **a bright high-acid cider** and **a traditional perry** rather than ordering three of the same generic "medium" draught.

**Qualification matters:** CAMRA's current real-cider criteria focus on **fermentation of fresh-pressed apple or pear juice**, not juice concentrate, or dilution after an alcohol-raising sugar addition. The container, colour or clarity cannot settle whether a cider qualifies. **Pasteurisation alone does not automatically disqualify** under the current guidance. "Real" and "**live**" are not synonyms; if you care about unpasteurised or naturally conditioned bottles, ask that *separately*. [CAMRA cider FAQ](https://camra.org.uk/promotional-campaigns/promoting-real-cider-and-perry/faqs) · [CAMRA producer guidance](https://camra.org.uk/producers) · [CAMRA on live cider/perry](https://members.camra.org.uk/learn-discover/the-basics/what-are-live-cider-and-perry/).

**Five productive questions at the bar:**

1. "**Who made it, and what is the exact product?**" A single producer may have both qualifying and non-qualifying lines.
2. "**Are there perries as well as ciders?**" The bottle/fridge list may be bigger than the taps.
3. "**Is it fresh-pressed juice rather than from concentrate?**" If the bartender doesn't know, ask to see producer information instead of assuming.
4. "**How dry, sharp or tannic is this one?**" These describe **different** characteristics; ask for a taster where available.
5. "**Is it live, filtered or pasteurised—and how has it been conditioned?**" This is an extra sensory question, not a substitute for CAMRA's real-cider definition.

## A four-day tasting passport (optional, not a drinking challenge)

Use **halves or thirds where available**, share samples, alternate with water and food, and stop whenever you prefer. A good day is a couple of memorable, fresh pints, not crossing off all the boxes.

| Travel day | A contrast to seek | Memory prompt |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mon 9**, if Marble is open when you reach town | Marble Pint / Manchester Bitter (if on), or a qualifying real cider | "Which detail of the sloping room did we nearly miss?" |
| **Tue 10 Manchester** | Traditional **mild** or local session bitter versus a modern cask pale | "Was the best pint in the grand room or the plain local?" |
| **Wed 11 Stockport** | A Robinsons bitter; **Old Tom 8.5%** only if its Nov 7 cask survived; a cider-versus-perry taste at Petersgate | "Did the brewery-house pint differ from an independent cellar?" |
| **Thu 12 Liverpool** | A Merseyside bitter in an old corridor pub, then an unfamiliar stout or true cider at Ship & Mitre | "What was different from Manchester's bitters?" |
| **Fri 13 Liverpool** | Something restrained in the Roscoe Head (if trading), then optional dark beer or producer-led cider | "Which beer was most worth slowing down for?" |

### A 30-second tasting note

Record **pub, date, beer/producer, actual dispense, ABV and portion**, then just four impressions: *aroma*, *body/carbonation*, *balance/finish* and *would order again?* For cider add *apple/pear variety if known*, *acidity*, *tannin* and *residual sweetness*. A one-line observation will be more informative after the trip than a bare numerical score.
